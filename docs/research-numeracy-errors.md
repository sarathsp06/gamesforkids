# Research Brief: Arithmetic Development & Error Meaning, Ages 4–8

## How arithmetic skill develops
- **Overlapping waves (Robert Siegler)**: children of one age use *multiple* strategies side by side — count-all from 1 ("sum"), count-on from the larger addend ("min"), derived facts (6+7 = 6+6+1), and direct retrieval — gradually shifting toward retrieval. A wrong answer often reveals which strategy was used, not just "doesn't know". [Siegler & Shrager 1984 / overlapping waves](https://siegler.tc.columbia.edu/wp-content/uploads/2019/12/4027Reading-ShragerSiegler-1998.pdf)
- Typical trajectory: 5-year-olds count from 1; ~7-year-olds count on from the larger addend; ~10-year-olds mostly retrieve. [Siegler & Shipley 1995](https://siegler.tc.columbia.edu/wp-content/uploads/2019/06/sieglershipley95.pdf)
- **Derived-fact strategies** (N±1 from a known fact) are a *good* sign in 5–8-year-olds, but misfire systematically in subtraction (e.g. using "subtrahend+1" wrongly), producing near-miss answers. [Dowker et al., J. Exp. Child Psych.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3880841)

## Error taxonomies
- Classic classification (Linda Cox, 744 children): **systematic** errors (consistent wrong procedure), **random** errors, and **careless slips** — systematic errors persist without intervention and are diagnostic. [Cox 1974, ERIC](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED092407.pdf)
- **"Buggy algorithms" (Brown & VanLehn)**: multidigit errors are mostly *correctly executed wrong procedures* — e.g. always subtracting smaller-from-larger digit (73−29 → 56), forgetting to carry, borrowing from the wrong column. These are rational "repairs" of an incomplete procedure, not randomness. [Brown & VanLehn 1982, "Towards a Generative Theory of Bugs"](https://siegler.tc.columbia.edu/wp-content/uploads/2019/12/4027Reading-Brown-VanLehn1982.pdf); [VanLehn, *Mind Bugs* (MIT Press)](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262220361/mind-bugs)
- **Fact-retrieval errors**: wrong answers in single-digit addition are usually *counting-string associates* (6+2→7 or 9, the neighbor in the count sequence) or *near table entries* (6+7→12 or 14; in multiplication, operand-related errors like 6×7→48). Geary showed low achievers retrieve counting-string neighbors — off-by-one is the signature retrieval error. [Geary et al. 2011, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3163113)
- **Off-by-one counting errors**: count-on strategies produce ±1 errors (starting count on the addend itself, or stopping a step early/late). Extremely common at 4–7 and indicate correct strategy with slip in execution. [Siegler, Strategy Choices](https://siegler.tc.columbia.edu/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/siegleradolphlem96.pdf)
- **Inattention slips**: in children with math difficulty, many multidigit errors are "switch" errors (adding instead of subtracting) and visual slips correlated with behavioral inattention, not conceptual gaps. [Raghubar et al. 2009, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2788949)

## Place value, digit reversal, transcoding
- Place-value mastery (knowing 47 = 4 tens + 7 ones) is a *stage-like* achievement across grades 2–5; failing it predicts chronic low achievement. Partial knowledge yields "smart errors" — expanded forms like writing 647 for "sixty-four". [Mix et al. 2015, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4460578); [Bower et al., "Smart Errors"](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10506169)
- **Dutch matters here**: Dutch number words are inverted ("vierenzestig" = four-and-sixty = 64). Zuber et al. found ~half of German-speaking (inverted-language) children's transcoding errors were pure inversion errors (writing 46 for 64); Dutch children show the same pattern vs. English peers. For a Dutch-language math game, 74-for-47 is an *expected linguistic* error, not a math failure. [Zuber et al. 2009](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022096508000519); [Krinzinger et al. 2014 (Dutch), PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3990049)
- **Mirror-writing single digits** (writing Ɛ for 3) at ages 5–6 reflects immature left-right orientation memory, not number misunderstanding; it self-resolves. [Fischer 2013, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23584491)

## Approximate number system (ANS) — why near answers show number sense
- Humans have an innate ANS encoding approximate magnitudes; its precision sharpens from ~3:4 ratio discrimination at age 3–4 toward 9:10 in adults. [Halberda & Feigenson 2008](https://panamath.org/papers/HalberdaFeigenson2008DevPsych.pdf); [Dehaene, *The Number Sense*]
- Preschool ANS precision predicts later school math (one study: 35% of variance in math at age 6). [Mazzocco, Feigenson & Halberda 2011, PLoS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0023749); [Libertus, Feigenson & Halberda 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3338171/)
- Implication: an answer of 14 to 6+7 is magnitude-plausible (good ANS + retrieval slip); 31 is magnitude-implausible (weak number sense or wild guess). Distance-from-correct, scaled by problem size (ratio, not absolute difference), is the research-grounded closeness metric.
- Caveat: ANS→achievement link weakens when controlling for general cognition; treat it as one signal, not a diagnosis. [Overview](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/20445911.2022.2044338)

## Timed fluency practice: pros and cons at 4–8
- **Against**: Jo Boaler (Stanford) argues timed tests trigger math anxiety in roughly a third of students, starting as early as grade 1–2; Beilock's work shows anxiety loads working memory and depresses performance. [Boaler 2014, *Teaching Children Mathematics*](https://pubs.nctm.org/view/journals/tcm/20/8/article-p469.xml); [Fluency Without Fear, youcubed](https://www.youcubed.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/09/Fluency-Without-Fear-1.28.15.pdf). Henry & Brown (2008) found frequent timed testing in ~300 first graders correlated with *lower* fact retrieval and number sense. [NCTM summary](https://www.nctm.org/uploadedFiles/Conferences_and_Professional_Development/Webinars_and_Webcasts/Webcasts/April27WebinarHandout2.pdf)
- **For / nuance**: the evidence that timing *causes* anxiety is contested; brief, low-stakes retrieval practice does build automaticity, and meta-analysis puts the anxiety–performance link at small-to-moderate (r≈−.28). Covert timing and complex problems raise anxiety most in already-anxious kids. [Barshay, KQED review](https://www.kqed.org/mindshift/61712/do-math-drills-help-children-learn); [Barroso et al. 2021 meta-analysis, Psych. Bulletin](https://psycnet.apa.org/doi/10.1037/bul0000307); [Caviola et al. 2024, J. School Psych.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsp.2024.101316)
- Consensus for 4–8: fluency goals yes, visible countdown clocks and public speed comparison no. Bay-Williams & Kling: assess fluency through games and observation, not timed tests. [Bay-Williams & Kling 2019, ASCD](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED592367)

## Mastery-learning thresholds
- Bloom's mastery learning: advance on ~80–90% criterion with corrective feedback loops; robust positive effects in synthesis studies. [Anderson 1994 synthesis, ERIC](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED382567.pdf)
- Fact-fluency criteria (precision-teaching / CBM tradition): ~20–40 correct digits/problems per minute written for primary grades, capped at ~2/3 of the child's motor writing speed — speed criteria must respect motor limits of young children. [Crawford, Mastering Math Facts research](https://mrbartonmaths.com/resourcesnew/8.%20Research/Fluency/Mastering%20Math%20Facts%20Research%20and%20Results.pdf); [Intervention Central CBM norms](https://www.interventioncentral.org/sites/default/files/workshop_files/allfiles/5_CBA_Math_Computation_Directions.pdf)
- Practical digital rule: "mastered" fact ≈ correct within ~2–3 s (retrieval speed, not counting speed) on repeated spaced encounters; keep a small set of facts in rotation, add new ones only when current set is ≥80–90% fast-correct (incremental rehearsal). [Taylor et al. 2023, Intervention in School & Clinic](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/10534512231175159)

## Wrong-answer pattern → likely cause → closeness score
| Pattern (example) | Likely cause | Score |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Off by 1 (6+7→12 or 14; 8−3→4) | Counting slip or counting-string retrieval neighbor (Siegler; Geary) | High (~0.8) — right strategy, execution slip |
| Off by 2, or ± one operand step in ×-tables (6×7→48, 36) | Near-neighbor retrieval; decent magnitude sense | Medium-high (~0.6) |
| Digit transposition (47→74), esp. in Dutch | Language inversion / transcoding, not arithmetic (Zuber; Krinzinger) | High (~0.8) in NL, medium in EN; flag as "digits swapped", never plain wrong |
| Single mirrored digit (Ɛ for 3) | Motor/orientation immaturity, age-normal ≤6 (Fischer) | Treat as correct with gentle note |
| Smaller-from-larger subtraction (73−29→56) | Buggy algorithm: no borrowing concept (Brown & VanLehn) | Medium (~0.5): systematic, needs reteach of borrowing, not "try again" |
| Carry forgotten (38+25→53) / carry added twice | Procedural slip in known algorithm | Medium-high (~0.6) |
| Wrong operation (6+2→4) | Attention switch error (Raghubar) | Medium (~0.4–0.5): prompt "look at the sign" |
| Magnitude-plausible but unpatterned (6+7→15) | ANS-guided estimate; retrieval not yet formed | Scale by ratio: score ≈ max(0, 1 − |ans−correct|/correct), floor at plausibility |
| Magnitude-implausible (6+7→31 or 2) | Guess / disengagement / weak number sense | Low (~0–0.2); drop difficulty rather than penalize harder |

## Implications for our games
1. Score closeness by **ratio distance** (|ans−correct|/correct), with pattern bonuses for off-by-one, transposition, and known buggy-algorithm outputs — each pattern earns distinct feedback text, since cause differs.
2. In Dutch mode, detect and softly credit digit-inverted answers; say "bijna — de cijfers staan omgedraaid".
3. No visible countdown for ages 4–6; use invisible response-time logging to classify retrieval (<3 s) vs counting. Introduce gentle, opt-in speed play only at 7–8.
4. Mastery gate: promote a fact/level at ≥80–90% correct with fast retrieval over spaced sessions; on systematic bugs (same wrong procedure 3×), switch to a teaching moment instead of repeating the drill.
5. Keep near-miss feedback warm: a close answer is evidence of number sense (ANS), and derived-fact misfires are a developmental step, not failure.
